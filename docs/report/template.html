What we built

The system, the engineering choices behind it, and the measured results, with caveats.

A1The pitch in one paragraph

We gave Gemini 4 the job of an analog circuit designer. The circuit is a two-stage op-amp on SkyWater's open 130 nm process (SKY130). It must meet four specs (phase margin, bandwidth, gain and current) at 15 combinations of manufacturing corner and temperature. A Gemini 4 Carbon architect reads the circuit description and every result so far, writes a diagnosis in circuit terms, and gives four Gemini 4 Hydrogen workers different search directions. The workers propose concrete transistor sizes. The ngspice circuit simulator judges every proposal at all 15 points, so the models never decide pass or fail. With the same budget of 40 candidates (600 simulations), the swarm found a passing design in {{k_sw_ng}} of {{n_sw_ng}} runs on the bare spec and {{k_sw_g}} of {{n_sw_g}} runs with a safety guard band. A2The pipeline

A deck is one ngspice input file: the circuit, one process corner, one temperature and the measurements. One candidate is one full set of transistor sizes, judged with 15 decks. Every method gets 40 candidates, and the published starting design counts as candidate 1 for every method.

  1. Inputs. The circuit comes from AMPICBench, a public op-amp benchmark (CC BY-NC 4.0) that ships SKY130 netlists, loop-gain testbenches and published results. We reproduce its published typical-corner numbers (60.59 dB, 125.4 MHz, 31.5° against 60.6 dB, 126 MHz, 31.5°). A short "circuit card" in the prompt explains each transistor's role and the bias rules.
  2. Architect (gemini-4-carbon, thinking level medium). It sees the circuit card, the knob ranges, the spec and the best six designs so far. It returns a diagnosis and one search direction per worker. It runs once at the start and again, in the background, whenever the best score has not improved for two rounds.
  3. Workers (gemini-4-hydrogen, thinking level low, four in parallel). Each sees its direction, the three best designs and its own last four attempts, and returns two candidates as schema-checked JSON with a one-sentence rationale. Python clamps every number to the allowed range and drops duplicates.
  4. Judge (sizing/opamp2stg.evaluate). It writes 15 decks, runs them in parallel and parses loop gain, phase margin, unity-gain frequency, gain margin, core current and the saturation margin of all nine signal-path transistors. A budget counter checks before every deck.
  5. Re-check. The best design is simulated again at all 15 points with the full foundry library in a fresh evaluation.
  6. Sign-off agent (sizing/signoff.py). A Gemini Managed Agent (Antigravity agent with Carbon) gets the bare circuit, the schematic image and the spec, but none of our testbench code. It must write its own testbench, calibrate it against the published numbers, then verify the design at all 15 points through one tool, run_spice, which we execute locally.
  7. Visualizer (viz/report.py, viz/story.py). It produces the figures in this document and a projector-friendly story page.

A3Key engineering decisions, and why

1. A slim model library: 0.2 s per simulation instead of 9 s

The full SKY130 library loads every device type in the process. We cut it down to the four transistor types the op-amp uses and the five corners. Results match the full library exactly, and one candidate (15 decks in parallel) takes about 0.6 s. At that speed simulation is cheap, so the scarce resource is LLM calls, and the fair way to compare methods is the number of simulations. We still re-check every final design on the full library, so the speed trick cannot change a verdict.

2. Per-role knobs, with tail and sink length fixed

The original netlist gives every NMOS one shared width and length (and every PMOS another). We split the nine signal-path transistors into five roles (input pair, mirror load, tail, stage-2 driver, stage-2 sink) and gave each its own width, plus a length for the input pair, load and driver. With Ibias, Cc and Rz that makes 11 knobs. Matched devices read the same parameter, so matching comes for free. The tail and sink transistors copy current from a frozen bias network whose reference device is 5 µm × 0.3 µm. A current mirror only copies correctly when the lengths match, so their length stays at 0.3 µm and their width sets the current: Itail ≈ Ibias · 2 · Wtail / 5 µm and Isink ≈ Ibias · 3 · Wsink / 5 µm.

3. Saturation rules on every transistor at every corner

An amplifier transistor only amplifies when it is in saturation (Part B2). We require |VDS| − |VDSAT| ≥ 50 mV for the input pair, load and driver at all 15 points. The bottom devices of the tail and sink only have to be in saturation (≥ 0 mV), because the frozen bias network fixes their headroom: the published design gives them only 23–37 mV at the typical corner. An optimizer that skips this check can report a "pass" with a transistor in triode. That is the analog version of reward hacking.

4. A guard band, because a bare pass can be a pass by 0.5°

The best design of our first run (first40) passed with a worst-case phase margin of {{f40_pm}}° and a worst-case unity-gain frequency of {{f40_ugf}} MHz, against limits of 60° and 10 MHz. Model error, layout parasitics and random mismatch would eat that margin. So the optimizers now aim at a tighter design target: PM ≥ 65°, UGF ≥ 11 MHz, gain ≥ 63 dB, core current ≤ 90 µA, and 10 mV extra saturation margin. We report both "passes the target" and "passes the bare spec".

5. Budget counted in simulations, checked before every deck

Every method gets 40 candidates × 15 decks = 600 decks. The counter refuses a deck before it runs, not between rounds. (An audit of a published LLM sizing agent found it overshot its budget in 4 of 8 trials because the check ran between rounds.)

6. Carbon timeout and fallback

Carbon is the stronger planner but its latency varies. Our first architect call took {{f40_carbon}} s; later ones took {{lat_c_range}} s, against a median of {{lat_h_med}} s for a Hydrogen worker call. The architect now has a 120 s timeout, after which the same prompt goes to Hydrogen at thinking level high. Re-plans run in the background so workers never wait. The fallback fired in: {{fallback_runs}}.

7. Guards against the known ways sizing agents cheat

  • Signed spec parsing. AutoSizer (ICML 2026) checks specs with the regex (\w+)\s*([><=]+)\s*([\d.]+), which cannot match a minus sign, so a spec like gain > -5 is silently dropped. An independent audit reports this lifted reported success from about 69% to 93–95%; we confirmed the regex ourselves. Our AMS-SizingBench runner parses signs and fails any constraint whose metric is missing.
  • The LLM never judges. Pass or fail comes only from ngspice output. A failed simulation counts as a failure.
  • All corners, all devices. A typical-corner pass does not count (see the numbers in A4).
  • Feasible first. The final design is the lowest-current design among those that pass, never a high-score infeasible one.
  • Geometry clamped to SKY130 minimums (NMOS L ≥ 0.15 µm, PMOS L ≥ 0.35 µm, W ≥ 0.5 µm in our ranges).
  • Corner-name quirk. In this PDK build the corner labelled sf gives more NMOS current than tt, the opposite of its name. The prompt tells the models to reason from measured numbers, not corner names.

A5Independent sign-off by a Managed Agent

Our evaluator could share a bug with our optimizer. So a Gemini Managed Agent re-verifies the final design without our code. It gets the op-amp and bias subcircuits as a bare netlist (no supply, stimulus, load or measurements), the schematic image, and the spec. It must choose its own loop-gain method, first reproduce the circuit authors' published numbers on the published sizing (within 1 dB, 3% and 1.5°), and only then measure our design at all 15 points. Its single tool, run_spice, runs a deck on our machine with ngspice and the full SKY130 library; a filter blocks .lib, .include, .temp and shell lines so the agent cannot swap the model files. We compute our own verdict before the agent starts.

A7Honest caveats

  • Few runs. Three or four runs per method. We report k/n and per-run numbers, not percentages or significance tests.
  • One development circuit. The prompts were written while looking at it. Held-out circuits are the fix (A6).
  • The swarm gets a prior that the baselines do not. The circuit card tells the models what each transistor does and how the bias currents scale. That knowledge is the point of the method, but it means we compare "LLM with designer knowledge" against blind search. We did not test Bayesian optimization with physics priors (for example, warm-started from a hand design or searching in gm/ID space), which could close much of the gap.
  • Small budget. 40 candidates favors a method with priors. Blind BO may catch up at 300 or more; we did not run that.
  • "PVT" here means process and temperature. Supply voltage stays at 1.8 V. No Monte Carlo mismatch, no layout parasitics, no slew, noise, offset or rejection specs. This is schematic-level sizing.
  • Corner labels. sf and fs behave swapped relative to their names in this PDK build. We sweep all five, so the verdicts hold, but per-corner commentary should use the measured numbers.
  • Perception front end not built. The datasheet reader and the schematic role labeler (Decisions API) from the plan do not exist yet; the role map and the spec are hand-written. Multimodal input today is the schematic image given to the sign-off agent.
  • Hackathon rules unverified. We found no public page for this event; the rubric details came from the brief.
  • Cost estimate. Thinking tokens are billed as output in our estimate; prices are introductory and rise on 1 December.

Part B

Analog crash course

Enough circuit knowledge to explain the project and answer follow-ups. Every section ends with where it shows up in our numbers.

B1Why analog design is different

Digital design hides the transistor behind an abstraction: a wire is 0 or 1, a gate only has to switch cleanly, and a synthesis tool turns code into a netlist of standard cells. Analog circuits carry information in continuous voltages and currents, so the exact operating point of every transistor matters, along with how it drifts with temperature and manufacturing. No analog equivalent of logic synthesis exists. A designer picks a topology, chooses the width and length of every transistor and every bias current, and checks the result with a circuit simulator (SPICE). That choice of sizes is called sizing, and it is the job our system automates. Analog blocks take a small share of a mixed-signal chip's area but a large share of its design time and its test failures.

B2The transistor: a current source controlled by a voltage

A MOSFET has a gate, a source and a drain. The gate-source voltage VGS controls how much current ID flows from drain to source. Below the threshold voltage VTH almost nothing flows; above it, current grows quickly. The excess VGS − VTH is the overdrive VOV. The textbook "square law" says

ID ≈ ½ · μCox · (W/L) · VOV2

where W is the channel width, L the channel length and μCox a process constant. Wider means more current; longer means less. Treat the formula as intuition: modern devices deviate from it, which is why designers use simulation and lookup tables. NMOS transistors conduct with the gate above the source; PMOS transistors are the mirror image and sit on the supply side. Our op-amp uses SKY130's low-threshold (LVT) 1.8 V devices, nfet_01v8_lvt and pfet_01v8_lvt.

Saturation vs triode, and why the margin matters

For amplification a transistor must be in saturation: VDS greater than VDSAT (roughly the overdrive). There the drain current depends on the gate and hardly at all on the drain, which is what makes gain possible. If VDS drops below VDSAT, the device enters triode: it behaves like a resistor, its output resistance collapses, and the gain of its stage collapses with it. A current mirror in triode also stops copying its current correctly.

VDSAT and VDS both move with process corner and temperature. A device sitting 5 mV inside saturation at the typical corner can fall into triode at a cold, slow corner. So designers demand a margin |VDS| − |VDSAT| at every corner.

In this project: ≥ 50 mV for input pair, load and driver; ≥ 0 mV for tail and sink bottom devices; +10 mV with the guard band. The published design fails this: its tail cascode goes into triode at 125 °C in all five corners.

B3Small-signal quantities: gm, ro, intrinsic gain, gm/ID and L

An amplifier works on small wiggles around a fixed bias point. Three numbers describe a transistor at that point.

  • Transconductance gm = ΔID/ΔVGS: how much the current changes per volt on the gate. Units: siemens (A/V). More gm means more gain and more speed.
  • Output resistance ro: how little the current depends on the drain voltage (the slight slope in Figure 6). It grows with channel length L.
  • Intrinsic gain gm·ro: the most voltage gain one transistor can give, roughly 10 to 100 depending on process and L. A stage's gain is about gm times the resistance at its output.

gm/ID is the design variable modern analog designers use instead of raw width. It answers "how much gm do I get per microamp?" and it is set by how strongly the device is turned on.

gm/IDRegimeCharacterTypical use
15–25 /Vweak / moderate inversionefficient, low noise, little headroom needed; wide and slowinput pairs in low-power amps
≈10 /Vmoderate / strongbalancedsecond-stage driver
5–10 /Vstrong inversionfast, compact, better current matching; costs current and headroomcurrent mirrors, bias devices (often long L)

Length L trades gain against speed. A longer channel raises ro and so the intrinsic gain, and improves matching, but adds gate capacitance, which makes the device slower. gm/ID and L together fix a transistor's character; W then scales it to the current you need. That is why sizing can be done as arithmetic: spec → required gm → current → width.

In this project: the swarm lengthened the load and driver (0.7 µm → 1.0–1.85 µm) to raise the DC gain from 54.6 dB to above 63 dB, and widened the input pair (5 µm → 22–50 µm) to push it toward high gm/ID.

B4The two-stage Miller op-amp, block by block

An op-amp amplifies the difference between two inputs by a large factor. Used with feedback, that large gain turns into a precise, stable smaller gain. The two-stage Miller op-amp is the standard teaching and benchmarking circuit, and it is ours.

Total gain is the product of the two stage gains, roughly gm,in(ro,in ∥ ro,load) × gm,drv(ro,drv ∥ ro,sink). Two stages give enough gain (60 dB = 1000×) at a 1.8 V supply. The price is a second pole, which is why compensation is needed.

The tail and sink are wide-swing cascodes: two stacked transistors that act as a better current source than one. Stacking costs voltage headroom, and the "wide-swing" bias trick keeps the bottom device just barely in saturation to save that headroom. That is why our rules only ask those bottom devices to stay saturated (≥ 0 mV), not to keep 50 mV.

B5Feedback, loop gain, unity-gain frequency and phase margin

An op-amp almost always runs inside a feedback loop: part of the output goes back to the inverting input. Our testbench uses the simplest loop, a unity-gain follower (output wired straight to the inverting input), which is also the hardest case for stability. The loop gain T is the gain a signal sees going once around the loop. While |T| is large, feedback holds the output accurate.

  • DC loop gain: |T| at low frequency, in decibels (dB = 20·log10). 60 dB is a factor of 1000; it sets accuracy (here, a follower error of about 0.1%).
  • Unity-gain frequency (UGF): the frequency where |T| falls to 1 (0 dB). Above it feedback stops working, so UGF measures speed. For a follower it equals the op-amp's gain-bandwidth product (GBW).
  • Phase margin (PM): 180° minus the phase lag of T at the UGF. If the lag reaches 180° while |T| is still 1, the signal returns inverted twice, adds to itself, and the circuit oscillates.
  • Gain margin (GM): how far |T| is below 1 at the frequency where the lag reaches 180°. A second safety number.

The ringing intuition. Phase margin decides how the output answers a step. With about 60° it settles with a small overshoot, under 10%. At 45° it overshoots by roughly 20–25% and rings for a few cycles. At 30° it overshoots by about a third and rings for many cycles; near 0° it oscillates. The published design has 31.5° at the typical corner and {{def_pm}}° at its worst corner, so it rings badly. That is why 60° is the usual target, and why we added 5° of guard band on top.

How we measure it: AMPICBench's testbench uses Tian's double-injection method, which measures loop gain without breaking the loop, so the DC bias point stays correct. Our sign-off agent has to pick and justify its own method.

B6Compensation: pole splitting, the right-half-plane zero, and Rz

Each stage has a high-resistance node with some capacitance, and every such node makes a pole: a frequency where the gain starts to fall and the phase lag grows. Two poles at similar frequencies pile up nearly 180° of lag near the UGF. Miller compensation puts a capacitor Cc across the second stage. The second stage's gain multiplies Cc's effect at node X, which pushes the first pole down to a low frequency (the dominant pole) and the second pole up. This is pole splitting. The useful approximations are:

UGF ≈ gm,in / (2π·Cc)     second pole ≈ gm,drv / (2π·CL)     zero ≈ gm,drv / (2π·Cc)

For about 60° of phase margin the second pole must sit at least about 2.2× above the UGF. Cc also opens a feed-forward path from node X to the output, creating a right-half-plane (RHP) zero. Unlike a normal zero it adds phase lag, so it hurts stability. A resistor Rz in series with Cc, near 1/gm,drv, pushes that zero to infinity; a larger Rz moves it into the left half plane, where it adds helpful phase lead.

Headroom: why the tail drops into triode at cold, slow corners

The input common-mode voltage is fixed at 0.9 V. The tail's drain sits one input-pair VGS below it: Vtail ≈ 0.9 V − (VTH + VOV,in). At cold temperature and in slow corners, VTH is higher, so VGS rises and the tail node drops. The two stacked tail devices then share less voltage, and the bottom one (whose headroom the frozen bias already keeps small) falls out of saturation first. That is exactly what the published design shows (Figure 9, bottom): the tail bottom device goes into triode at −40 °C.

Two fixes, and the swarm used both. Widen the input pair: at the same current a wider device needs less overdrive (in the square law, 10× the width cuts VOV by about √10), so VGS drops and the tail node rises. Cut the tail current: less current also lowers VOV,in. Design #{{ex_n}}'s rationale says this in words: "widening W_in_um to {{ex_win}} um to protect tail saturation margin".

B8PVT corners and Monte Carlo: why "works at typical" is not enough

No two chips come out of the fab the same, and every chip runs at many temperatures. Designers check three kinds of variation.

  • Process corners. The foundry ships models for typical (tt), fast (ff) and slow (ss) transistors, and the mixed cases sf and fs, where the first letter is NMOS and the second PMOS. "Fast" means more current at the same voltages (lower threshold, higher mobility).
  • Voltage. The supply can sit about 10% high or low. We did not vary it: all our runs use 1.8 V.
  • Temperature. Typically −40 °C to 125 °C. Heat lowers mobility (slower) and lowers threshold; cold does the opposite.

Our 15 points are 5 corners × 3 temperatures. Corners bound global variation, where every transistor on a die shifts together. Monte Carlo simulation samples random local variation (mismatch between neighboring transistors) hundreds of times to estimate offset spread and yield. We did not run Monte Carlo; the guard band is a stand-in for that margin.

In this project: {{tt_pass}} of {{tt_n}} guard-band candidates passed at tt/27 °C, only {{all_pass}} of {{tt_n}} at all 15 points. Worst cases land in different places for different specs: for the best guard-band designs, phase margin bottoms out at fs/125 °C, UGF at ss/125 °C and saturation margin at fs/−40 °C.

B10Why sizing is hard to automate, and where LLMs fit

Sizing is constrained optimization. The variables are widths, lengths, bias currents and passives (11 here; hundreds in a real block). The constraints are the specs at every corner plus the operating region of every device. The objective is usually power or area. The problem is non-convex, each evaluation is a batch of simulations, and the feasible region can be tiny.

ApproachHow it worksWeakness
Hand designDesigner knows which knob moves which spec; gm/ID chartsSlow; needs scarce experts
Equation-basedWrite performance as formulas; solve exactly (e.g. geometric programming, 2001)Formulas drift from reality in modern processes
Black-box searchRandom, genetic, Bayesian optimization (BO), RL with SPICE as oracleBlind to physics; needs many simulations; corners multiply cost
LLM agentsModel reasons like a designer, simulator verifiesImprecise with numbers; can "declare success"; needs a hard judge

Bayesian optimization fits a statistical surrogate (a Gaussian process, GP) to the results so far and picks the next point where improvement looks most likely. TPE (tree-structured Parzen estimator, Optuna's default) does the same with density models instead of a GP. Both see only 11 numbers and a score. They do not know that Cc trades bandwidth for phase margin, or that the tail needs headroom.

The division of labor. The 2025–2026 papers converge on one split: the LLM does what looks like a designer's reasoning (what each device does, which knob to move, which direction, why a corner fails), and a simulator plus, optionally, a numerical optimizer does the numbers and the judging. AutoSizer (ICML 2026) uses an LLM to reshape the search space around a numerical optimizer. Our system keeps the LLM proposing concrete sizes, but only ngspice decides pass or fail. The one independent rerun we found shows plain TPE beating a published LLM agent once the agent's spec check was fixed, so we claim only what we measured: fewer simulations to an all-corner pass on this circuit, at this budget, with reasons attached to every move.

Judge questions, glossary and pitch

Short answers you can say out loud, each with a number from this project.

C1Likely judge questions

Why is phase margin important?

An op-amp runs inside feedback. If the loop's phase lag reaches 180° while the loop gain is still 1, the feedback adds instead of subtracts and the circuit oscillates. Phase margin is the distance from that point. At 60° a step settles with under 10% overshoot; at 30° it rings. The published design we start from has {{def_pm}}° at its worst corner. Every swarm design we report has at least 60°, and the guard-band runs at least 65°.

What is a PVT corner?

Process, voltage, temperature: the manufacturing and operating extremes a chip must survive. Process corners are fast or slow NMOS and PMOS from the foundry's models; temperature runs −40 to 125 °C. We check 5 process corners × 3 temperatures = 15 points for every candidate. We hold supply voltage at 1.8 V; adding ±10% supply is on the next-steps list.

Why not just use Bayesian optimization?

We did, as the baseline, at the same 600-simulation budget. TPE found no passing design in {{n_bl_g}} guard-band runs; on the bare spec only {{k_bl_ng}} of {{n_bl_ng}} finished baseline runs passed, and only near the end of the budget. The same samplers succeed on easier benchmark circuits ({{ams_k}} of {{ams_n}} runs on AMS-SizingBench at the typical corner), so the harness is fine. The 15-corner feasible region is a thin sliver, and BO is blind to the physics that points at it. BO with a larger budget or physics priors may close the gap; we did not test that, and the best system is probably a hybrid.

How do you know the LLM didn't cheat?

The LLM never judges. ngspice measures every candidate at all 15 points, including the saturation of all nine signal-path transistors; a failed simulation is a failure; the budget counter blocks extra simulations; the final design is re-simulated on the full foundry library. We also guard against a published failure: AutoSizer's spec parser silently dropped negative thresholds and inflated success from about 69% to over 90%. And a separate Managed Agent re-derives the numbers with its own testbench.

Did it just copy the published design?

The published design is our starting point and it fails every spec (PM {{def_pm}}°, gain {{def_gain}} dB, {{def_icore}} µA, a device in triode). The passing designs are far from it: input pair 5 µm → 22–50 µm wide, Cc 0.25 pF → 0.5–1.35 pF, tail width 5 µm → about 1 µm.

Is three seeds enough?

No, not for statistics. That is why we show every run instead of a percentage. The swarm passed in all {{n_sw_g}} guard-band runs and all {{n_sw_ng}} bare-spec runs; TPE passed in none of its finished runs. The gap is large, but the honest claim is "on this circuit, at this budget". Held-out circuits are the next test.

Why two models?

Carbon is the stronger planner and writes the diagnosis; Hydrogen is faster (median {{lat_h_med}} s per call) and cheaper for the many proposal calls. Carbon's latency varied from {{lat_c_range}} s and once hit {{f40_carbon}} s, so it has a 120 s timeout with a Hydrogen fallback, and re-plans run in the background.

What is the guard band for?

Our first run's best design passed with {{f40_pm}}° of phase margin against a 60° limit. Model error, layout and mismatch would erase that. The optimizers now aim 5° higher on PM, 10% higher on UGF, 3 dB higher on gain, 10% lower on current and 10 mV more saturation margin. It costs current ({{icore_sw_g_range}} µA instead of {{icore_sw_ng_range}} µA) and takes longer to find.

How do you know the fast model library gives the right answer?

We built it by trimming the official library, checked that it gives identical numbers, and re-simulate every final design on the full library (about 9 s per deck instead of 0.2 s). Every passing swarm design re-passed.

Where is the multimodality?

Today the sign-off agent reads the schematic image along with the bare netlist, and the visualizer renders schematics and Bode plots for people. The planned perception front end (datasheet PDF → spec, schematic image → device roles) is not built yet; we say so rather than fake it.

What does the sign-off agent add if you already simulate?

Independence. Our optimizer and our evaluator share code, so a bug in the testbench would fool both. The agent gets no testbench, must calibrate its own against the circuit authors' published numbers, and then verify the design. If its verdict matches ours, the testbench is unlikely to be the problem.

Why does the tail get 0 mV saturation margin while others need 50 mV?

The tail and sink are wide-swing cascodes biased by a fixed network we may not change. That network deliberately runs their bottom device just inside saturation (23–37 mV at the typical corner in the published design). Demanding 50 mV there would be impossible without redesigning the bias network, so the rule is "stay saturated" (plus 10 mV with the guard band).

What does it cost, and how long does it take?

{{cost_range}} of Gemini calls per 40-candidate run ({{calls_range}} calls), and about {{wall_sw}} s of wall time on a laptop. Simulation is cheap (0.6 s per candidate), so LLM latency dominates.

Would this work on a real product?

Not as is. Real blocks have hundreds of devices, need supply corners, Monte Carlo yield, noise and slew specs, and post-layout verification. This is the schematic-sizing step of that flow, with an honest judge. The parts that carry over are the role-based knobs, the all-corner judge and the guard band.

What is core current, and why minimize it?

The current through the tail and the second-stage sink: the part of the supply current the knobs control (the fixed bias network is excluded). Lower current means lower power, which is what a datasheet's quiescent-current line measures. Once the spec is met, the swarm's only objective is lowering it.

C2Glossary

AC / stability analysis. Small-signal simulation over frequency; gives gain and phase.

AMPICBench. Public op-amp benchmark with SKY130 netlists and published results; source of our circuits.

Bias. The fixed DC currents and voltages that put each transistor at its operating point.

Bode plot. Gain (dB) and phase against log frequency.

Cascode. A second transistor stacked on the first to raise output resistance; costs headroom.

Cc, Rz. Miller compensation capacitor and its series nulling resistor.

CL. Load capacitance at the output; 500 fF here.

Core current. Tail + stage-2 sink current; our power objective.

Corner. A foundry model for fast/slow NMOS/PMOS: tt, ff, ss, sf, fs.

Current mirror. Transistors that copy a reference current; needs matched lengths.

dB. 20·log10 of a voltage ratio; 60 dB = 1000×.

Deck. One ngspice input file: circuit, corner, temperature, measurements.

Differential pair. Two matched transistors that amplify the difference between two inputs.

Dominant pole. The low-frequency pole Cc creates on purpose so gain falls smoothly.

Gain margin. How far loop gain is below 1 where the phase reaches −180°.

gm. Transconductance: change in drain current per volt of gate voltage.

gm/ID. Transconductance per unit current; sets a device's efficiency and speed.

Guard band. Tighter internal target than the spec, to leave margin.

Headroom. Voltage left for a device to stay in saturation.

Intrinsic gain. gm·ro, the most gain one transistor can give.

Loop gain. Gain around a feedback loop; its UGF and phase set speed and stability.

LVT. Low-threshold-voltage transistor variant.

Managed Agent. A Gemini agent run by Google with its own sandbox and tools; ours calls back to local ngspice.

Monte Carlo. Many simulations with random device mismatch, to estimate yield.

Netlist. Text list of components and connections that SPICE reads.

ngspice. Open-source SPICE circuit simulator (version 47 here).

Operating point. The DC voltages and currents of every device with no signal.

Overdrive VOV. VGS − VTH.

PDK. Process design kit: the foundry's models and rules. SKY130 is open.

Phase margin. 180° minus loop phase lag at the UGF; ≥ 60° is the usual target.

Pole / zero. Frequencies where gain starts to fall (pole) or rise (zero), each with a phase shift.

PVT. Process, voltage, temperature variation.

ro. Output resistance of a saturated transistor; grows with L.

RHP zero. Right-half-plane zero from Cc's feed-forward path; adds lag. Rz fixes it.

Saturation. Region where a transistor acts as a current source; needed for gain.

SKY130. SkyWater's open 130 nm CMOS process.

Slew rate. Fastest output swing for a large step; not in our spec.

Tail. Current source under the differential pair.

Tian method. Loop-gain measurement that injects voltage and current without breaking the loop.

TPE / GP-BO. Bayesian-style samplers in Optuna; our blind baselines.

Triode. Region where a transistor acts as a resistor; gain collapses.

UGF / GBW. Frequency where loop gain falls to 1; equals gain-bandwidth for a follower.

VDSAT. Minimum drain-source voltage for saturation.

VTH. Threshold voltage; rises in the cold and in slow corners.

Wide-swing cascode. Cascode biased to keep the bottom device just saturated, saving headroom.

C3The 60-second pitch

Every chip has analog blocks, and sizing their transistors is still done by hand by scarce experts. We made Gemini 4 do that job.

Our circuit is a two-stage op-amp on SkyWater's open 130-nanometer process. It has to be stable, fast, accurate and low-power at fifteen combinations of manufacturing corner and temperature. The published sizing fails: its phase margin is {{def_pm}} degrees, so it rings.

Gemini 4 Carbon plays the lead designer. It reads the results and writes a diagnosis in circuit terms, like "the tail transistor runs out of headroom at cold corners." Four Gemini 4 Hydrogen workers each propose sizes along a different direction. The models never grade themselves: the ngspice simulator checks every proposal at all fifteen corners, including whether each transistor is in saturation.

With 600 simulations, the swarm found a passing design in every run, {{k_sw_g}} of {{n_sw_g}} with a safety guard band. Bayesian-style optimizers with the same budget found none. Every swarm design re-passes on the full foundry models, and a separate Gemini Managed Agent writes its own testbench to double-check us. Each run costs under a dollar.
