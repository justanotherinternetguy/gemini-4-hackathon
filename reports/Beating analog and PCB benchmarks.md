# Repair Freerouting's sixteen failures with Gemini

The claim you can defend by 5:00 PM is narrow and still good: **the first Gemini result on PCBWorld-Bench, and a repair swarm that raises D3-A clean pass from 83 of 99 (your own Freerouting run) to N of 99, shown next to a best-of-N Freerouting control with no agents.** At **86 of 99** you are above every headline method in the PCBWorld paper (best: PPO at 0.86). You are not at the state of the art, and the pitch must say so: PCBRouteAgent, published 5 November, reports **0.949 on D3-A and 1.000 on D3-B** with Claude Opus 5.5, so beating it needs 95 of 99, which means fixing 12 of your 16 failed boards. Do not plan around that. Build in this order: the Freerouting control first, then a scripted repair with no model, then the swarm (Decisions API triages each failed board from its image, Carbon picks which nets to rip up and in what order, Hydrogen workers execute through PCBWorld's partial re-route, the official scorer grades the saved files). The coordination ideas with measured support are a planner over a fixed geometric executor, retries with selection, and diversity between workers; debate and extra roles have none. If the swarm adds nothing over the control, report that, and fall back on the analog evidence already in hand (3 of 3 seeds against GP-BO's 1 of 3 on the development op-amp, and 0 of 12 on held-out circuits). No one has yet shown that Gemini 4 can fix even one of the 16 boards, so treat every board count below as a target, not a forecast.

## The published bar moved to 0.949 on 5 November

The numbers in the team handoff are real but out of date, and they are best-of-5 values. PCBWorld (LG AI Research, 679 real boards) scores **clean pass**: the share of boards that are fully connected with zero error-level DRC violations ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915); [METRICS.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/LGAI-Research/PCBWorld/main/docs/METRICS.md)). Its headline table routes each board five times and keeps the rollout with the largest potential gain; a second table gives single-run values, which are much lower.

Three facts shape the claim. First, **no Gemini model appears in either paper**, so any scored Gemini run is a first ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915); [PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Second, the board arithmetic is fixed: from 83, you need **+3 boards to reach 0.86, +11 to tie 0.949 and +12 to beat it**. Third, the other tiers offer little today. D3-B (10 boards) is already at 1.000 and can only be tied; D3-C (10 boards) is open, with a best of 0.400 against a human reference of 0.900, but Freerouting passes only 0.300 there and you have no run on it ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)).

One piece of good news: the human reference passes every D3-A board, so all 16 failures are routable in principle. That was not true of your analog held-out circuits, where nobody knew whether the specification was reachable. It also creates a leak risk. The human-routed board sits in the same dataset folder as the unrouted one, so copy only the unrouted board, the `.kicad_pro` and the `.dsn` into the swarm's working folder.

## Build order: control first, then scripted repair, then the swarm

The design follows from what the published agents could not do. PCBWorld's own LLM agents cannot delete copper, and the paper names this as the reason they never escape congestion once it forms; it names net order as the main cause of failure ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). Your environment removes that limit. It loads a routed board and re-routes only a chosen subset of nets (`target_nets`, `preserve_nontarget_routing`, `keep_nets`), and KiCad's push-and-shove router executes the actions. **So the model's job is small and specific: choose which nets to rip up, in what order to re-route them, and which nets to lock.** Freerouting does the rest in about 7 seconds per board ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)).

### Four rows, each one a control for the next

| Row | What runs | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Freerouting, one run | Already done: 83 of 99 | Same-day, same-machine baseline |
| 2. Freerouting, best of N | Re-run the 16 failures N times across 2 or 3 settings (`-mp` 10, 30, 100; snap-angle patch on and off) | How much is free without any agent |
| 3. Scripted repair, no model | Rip up the open nets plus nets whose copper crosses the open connection; re-route with `net_select`, `start_route`, `finish`, `net_end` in a fixed order | What the partial re-route harness gives without Gemini |
| 4. Swarm | Row 3's harness, with Gemini choosing the rip-up set, the order and the locks | What coordination adds |

Row 2 is not optional. Best-of-5 alone is worth 5 points for Freerouting in the paper (0.75 to 0.80) ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). The research estimate for your boards, which is a guess and not a measurement, is that best-of-N recovers 2 to 5 of the 16 and agent repair adds 1 to 3. That means row 2 alone could cross 0.86. If it does, the headline still holds, but the swarm's contribution is only the gap between rows 2 and 4. Give row 2 the same total number of attempts per board that the swarm uses. Row 3 is cheap because it is the harness's first smoke test, and it has a published parallel: PCBRouteAgent without its LLM planner falls from 0.949 to 0.535 on D3-A ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)).

### One job per model

| Job | Model | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triage and blocker ranking | `gemini-decision-1` | Board image with open connections and violations drawn, plus the DRC report | Per board, a `choice`: rule artefact / blocked connection / needs a layer change. Per candidate net, a yes/no "is this net blocking the open connection?" with a probability |
| Planner | `gemini-4-carbon`, medium thinking | The same image, the DRC report, the Decisions probabilities, and what earlier attempts tried | Three or four different plans per board: rip-up set, re-route order, nets to lock, layer hint |
| Workers | `gemini-4-hydrogen`, low thinking, at least 4,096 output tokens | One plan each, the engine's text observation, and rejected-action notes | Engine actions: `finish` first, and `make_line` or `make_via` waypoints only after a rejection |
| Independent sign-off | Managed Agent (`antigravity-preview-09-2026`) | The saved boards and their `.kicad_pro` files | Its own DRC through a function tool that runs stock KiCad 10.0.8, and an agreement table against the official scorer |

This split matches Google's stated pairing, "Carbon plans and orchestrates, Hydrogen executes", with Hydrogen recommended for parallel subagents ([Schmid roundup](https://www.philschmid.de/google-deepmind-roundup-nov-2026)). No Google guidance on swarm coordination beyond that pairing was found. Three constraints come from your own measurements today. Carbon re-plans timed out at 120 seconds under API concurrency, so call Carbon once per board up front, cap it at four concurrent calls, and fall back to Hydrogen after 60 seconds. Gemini 4 spends a small output limit before it answers, so never run a worker below 4,096 tokens. And PCBWorld's conda environment pins `google-genai` 2.9.0 while the Decisions API needs 3.2 or later ([Decisions codelab](https://codelabs.developers.google.com/getting-started-with-gemini-decisions-api)), so run the engine as a small local service with one call, `reroute(board, rip_up, order, lock, waypoints)`, which returns the saved file path, the DRC summary and a rendered image. The swarm calls it from the project's own virtual environment.

The Decisions API earns its place because it answers up to 1,000 questions in one call in 150 to 250 ms with calibrated probabilities, at $0.10 per million input tokens ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemini-decisions-api/)). Use the probabilities for two decisions: which boards get worker time first, and which nets enter the rip-up set. Log every probability against the outcome, so you can show whether the screen predicted which boards flipped.

Images are an input the system uses, not decoration: the planner and the screen both read the board render that `pcb/live_view.py` already produces. The evidence that pictures help is thin. PCBRouteAgent's planner reads board images but reports no image ablation ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Your own analog test found role labelling at 46 of 49 with the schematic image against 43 of 49 from the netlist alone. Run the triage question with and without the image on the 16 boards and report the difference, whichever way it goes.

For the Managed Agents prize, reuse `sizing/managed.py`. It already drives a hosted agent that pauses on your own function tools, and that mechanism is documented ([Antigravity agent docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/antigravity-agent)). The sign-off agent is the safest use, because the same pattern reproduced your analog judge within 0.0001 dB today. A read-only DRC in KiCad 10 never writes the board, and it turns "different DRC build" into a measured agreement rate. If the plain worker loop is flipping boards by 15:30, wrap one worker as a Managed Agent with `reroute` as its tool. Do not spend the remaining time building the engine inside the hosted sandbox; the handoff still lists that as unverified, and the function-tool route does not need it.

## Planner-over-executor and retries have evidence; debate does not

The strongest evidence is in the routing papers themselves. Adding an LLM planner on top of a fixed geometric executor moved D3-A clean pass from **0.535 to 0.949** and D3-B from 0.000 to 1.000; the planner decides topology, order, corridors and layer changes, failures come back as structured feedback, and accepted routes are kept ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Keeping the engine in the loop matters as much: GPT-5.4 leaves 34.58 violations per board when it generates without the engine, 16.28 in plan-then-execute mode and 1.31 in interactive mode ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). Retries with selection are worth 5 points for Freerouting, 18 for PPO and 23 for GPT-5.4 ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). PCBRouteAgent's recovery is bounded, with 12 replans per board, four per connection and one rip-up per obstructing net ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Copy the bounds as well as the idea.

The sizing literature points the same way, and the mapping to routing is direct. A hybrid, where the LLM proposes some points and a numeric optimizer proposes the rest, was the only method to meet every spec on two circuits at 105 simulations ([ADO-LLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18770v1)). That is the argument for Freerouting plus a planner, not a model routing from scratch. Removing the outer loop that changes the search space halved success, from 100% to 50%, in AutoSizer's ablation ([AutoSizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02849v1)); in routing, changing the search space means changing the rip-up set. Diversity has support too: one region per round "hindered exploration" in HOLLM, and weighted parent sampling beat hill climbing in ShinkaEvolve, though both report this without numeric tables ([HOLLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.21372v1); [ShinkaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19349v1)). So give each worker a different plan, never the same one. Debate between agents has no support here. The only evidence found is negative, on question-answering tasks, and it was read from a search summary only ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)). When the checker answers in seconds, let the checker decide.

Your own analog results add the caution. The swarm passed 3 of 3 seeds at 40 candidates on the development op-amp, with and without a guard band, where Optuna GP-BO passed 1 of 3 (0 of 3 with the guard band), TPE and random search 0 of 3, and a single Gemini call 1 of 3. On two held-out cascode op-amps it passed **0 of 12 runs**, always stopped by one constraint, PMOS cascode saturation. Coordination did not rescue a search that never aimed at the binding constraint. The routing equivalent is to point every plan at the specific open connection or violation on that board, which is why triage comes before planning.

## Fallbacks: what the analog work can and cannot support

If the routing swarm yields nothing, the analog track still supports one honest claim: at an equal budget of 40 candidates across 15 PVT corners, the swarm beat GP-BO, TPE, random search and a single Gemini call on one circuit over three seeds, and an independent Managed Agent reproduced the judge within 0.0001 dB. It does not support a generalization claim. The 0 of 12 has useful published context, though. On OPERA's AMPICBench table, once a saturation-margin rule is added, TPE and random search pass **0 of 3 seeds on the folded-cascode two-stage op-amp**, and 0 of 3 and 1 of 3 on the telescopic two-stage; OPERA itself reports its weakest cascode sitting 23 to 63 mV into triode at one corner ([OPERA](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.39395)). Your held-out failure is on the constraint the literature already marks as the hard one. OPERA's specs and parameterization differ from yours, so cite this as context, not as a comparison.

AMS-SizingBench offers one narrow target and many traps. AutoSizer's paper reports 100% success in every tier at 300 samples and three trials, at the typical corner only, with no per-circuit numbers ([AutoSizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02849v1)). An independent audit found that its success check silently drops negative thresholds, and that plain Optuna TPE truly meets every spec in 67% of runs at 100 simulations ([audit](https://github.com/taha-mazher/analog-sizing-agent-audit)). Recounting that audit's per-circuit files shows TPE at 25 of 30 on the six advanced amplifiers at 100 simulations ([audit results](https://github.com/taha-mazher/analog-sizing-agent-audit/tree/main/results)). The only circuit with real headroom is **iac: TPE passes 2 of 5 seeds at 100 simulations**, GP-BO 2 of 5, random 1 of 5. If `bench/ams_swarm.py` already runs, five seeds on iac against a local TPE re-run is a 20-minute side result. Do not start it otherwise.

Analog layout supports no claim today. Your planner outputs finger counts, matched-pair patterns and a floorplan and stops before GDS, so no verifier can score it. The published results explain why that matters: on AnalogLayoutWorld, 91.1% of attempts produce GDS and 23.0% meet the acceptance criteria ([AnalogLayoutWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16991)), and CASCoDe reports DRC- and LVS-clean routed layouts on all 20 of its SKY130 amplifiers ([CASCoDe](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11710)). Show the planner as work in progress, with no number attached.

## What to disclose, and what not to claim

Put these disclosures on the results slide and in the README. The method is a hybrid: Freerouting routes the boards and Gemini repairs 16 of them, so it is not like-for-like with the GPT-5.4 agent that routes from scratch. The repaired boards are in the reported set, because D3-A's train and test lists are the same 99 boards; name the three development boards and give the result on the 13 untouched ones separately. State the attempts per board, the selection rule, and the wall-clock and token cost, next to the published costs of 7 seconds for Freerouting, 231 seconds for GPT-5.4 and 27 seconds for PCBRouteAgent on Haiku ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915); [PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Final numbers must come from PCBWorld's own evaluator on KiCad 9.0.8; label any KiCad 10 number as a proxy and give the agreement rate. The 0.84 baseline is one run on repository version 1.2.2, scored by the runner and not yet by the three-stage pipeline, while the published values are 0.80 (best of 5, mean of four seeds) and 0.75 (single run). One board is one percentage point, so a three-board gain is inside the spread between your baseline and the published one. List the excluded board (`0096_karabas-nano_wifi_revA`) and every board the swarm failed. The official count ignores dangling copper, so report the stricter count that includes it as well ([eval README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/LGAI-Research/PCBWorld/main/eval/README.md)). Cite PCBRouteAgent and the 0.94 ablation row as higher published numbers, in the same sentence as your own.

Do not claim the state of the art on PCBWorld, or anything about D3-B or D3-C from a demo run. Do not say the swarm "beats the GPT-5.4 agent" without the word hybrid. Do not say images help unless your own ablation shows it. Do not call the workers a Managed Agent swarm if they are plain API calls; say which parts are Managed Agents. Do not claim that scheduled Carbon re-plans improve anything, because they timed out and were never tested. Do not claim the analog swarm generalizes (0 of 12 held-out), that you beat AutoSizer (its reported rate is 100% by a flawed check at a different budget), or that you beat LEDRO, EE-Sizer or ADO-LLM, whose only SKY130 numbers are AutoSizer's re-implementations ([AutoSizer](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02849v1)). Do not turn 16 boards or three seeds into percentages without the counts beside them.

## Conclusion

The two tracks taught the same lesson from opposite sides. In analog sizing the swarm won where the search was open and lost where one constraint blocked every candidate. In routing, a classic tool already solves 84% of the problem, and what is left is 16 specific blockages. The swarm's worth is therefore measured on a small, named set of hard cases, and the control rows decide whether that worth is real. A four-row table in which best-of-N Freerouting takes most of the gain is still a credible submission. A single row that hides the control is not.

The unusual asset here is the partial re-route. Published LLM routing agents either route from scratch or cannot remove copper; your harness lets a model make the one decision that the papers identify as decisive, which is what to tear up and in what order. If that produces even two or three clean boards beyond the control, with the official scorer and an independent sign-off agent agreeing, you have a result no one has published on a Gemini model, and a method that carries over to the medium and hard tiers after the hackathon.
