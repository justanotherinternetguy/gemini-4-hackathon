# Fan out repairs; let KiCad judge

Run the next two hours as a shallow tree, not a swarm of peers: one board is one independent job, each board gets up to 16 separate repair attempts on separate board copies, PCBWorld's own scorer picks the winner, and Managed Agents work beside that loop, never inside it. This is the shape with the most measured support in 2026: a strong planner hands self-contained tasks to workers that never talk to each other, and a separate actor verifies ([Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases); [Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v2)). Gemini Managed Agents cannot spawn subagents, so your local process must be the orchestrator; the hosted agents earn their place as three bounded jobs that call your engine through client-executed tools: a deep-repair specialist for the one or two boards the swarm leaves nearly finished, an independent sign-off on the boards you claim, and an optional audit of your scoring glue ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/custom-agents.md)). The target is **5 of 10 clean boards**: PCBRouteAgent with Claude Opus 5.5 passes 4 of 10, Freerouting 3 of 10, and the human reference 9 of 10 ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Your structural edge is real: PCBRouteAgent's authors say its hard-tier failures come from never revisiting an accepted route, and your tool can rip up any net ([PCBRouteAgent project page](https://chip-ai-agent.github.io/research/pcbrouteagent/)). The gain is unproven: nobody has published an LLM repairing Freerouting output on this tier, and no Gemini result exists. Three code facts need action before the counted run: the Decisions API is named in `pcb/repair/swarm.py` but never called, each planning round waits up to 170 seconds for Carbon before any Hydrogen plan runs, and engine calls have no timeout.

## Why this shape: the evidence for each choice

**A tree with independent workers is the best-supported structure.** Cursor's flat design, with equal agents sharing a locked file, collapsed: "Twenty agents would slow down to the effective throughput of two or three." What scaled to hundreds of agents was planners that create tasks and workers that take one task and never coordinate with each other ([Cursor, January](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents); [Cursor, February](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)). A controlled study of 180 configurations explains when this pays. Multi-agent setups average **3.5% worse** than a single agent, gain **80.9%** on a task that splits into independent parts, and lose **39% to 70%** on sequential planning ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v2)). Ten boards are independent. The nets on one board are not: one agent's shove invalidates another's plan. So the split is by board and by attempt.

**Best-of-N with an exact judge is where width pays.** On PCBWorld's easy tier, five attempts with selection add 5 points for Freerouting, 18 for the reinforcement-learning agent and 23 for GPT-5.4 ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). Clean pass means fully connected with zero error-level rule violations and nothing else, so the scorer's output is already the repair worklist (PCBWorld `eval/metrics.py`, line 465, read today). The same fact makes the equal-attempt control mandatory: without it, the selection gain is credited to the agents.

**The model should choose and the router should draw.** PCBRouteAgent keeps the model out of geometry and calls it only when execution is stuck. On the hard tier the same executor scores **0 of 10 with no model, 1 of 10 with Claude Haiku 5.5 and 4 of 10 with Opus 5.5**, so planner quality is the lever there ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Control at the level of single steps is the expensive failure: PCBWorld's step agent used 676.6 model calls and 4,566 seconds per hard board for 0 of 10 ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Plans committed without engine feedback also fail: GPT-5.4 scored 0.19 in plan-then-execute mode against 0.65 interactively ([PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915)). Your plan format, a rip-up set, an order and a router mode, is the right grain.

**Rip-up of accepted routes is the published gap.** PCBRouteAgent's six failed hard boards are incomplete, not rule-breaking: mean routability is 0.949, and in the published case 70 of 83 connections were made because sensor power was routed first and trapped two signals. Its recovery allows one rip-up per obstructing net and never revisits accepted routes ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). Freerouting's failures have the opposite profile, 0.918 routability with **32 error violations per board**, and it uses half the vias of the human reference (21.4 against 44.0). Expect your failed boards to need both violation repair and a few forced layer changes. Take the violations-only boards first; they need the least work per clean board gained.

**Fast workers must not wait for the slow planner.** Hydrogen answers in about 8 seconds and Carbon in 40 to 150; Carbon timed out at 120 seconds whenever several jobs hit the API together, and scheduled re-plans never landed (measured today on the analog swarm). The literature describes the same bottleneck. Anthropic's lead agent blocks while it waits for a batch of subagents, and Cursor's synchronous cycle ran at the speed of its slowest worker ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system); [Cursor, February](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases)). The cost evidence favours the same split. With a frontier planner and cheap workers, Cursor reached the same final quality for **$1,339 against $10,565** for an all-frontier swarm, because workers consume at least 69% of the tokens ([Cursor, July](https://cursor.com/blog/agent-swarm-model-economics)).

**Managed Agents belong off the inner loop.** The red-team run today took a median of **58.9 seconds per client round trip**, and the two complete runs took 628 and 2,287 seconds (`runs/redteam/rt1/interactions.jsonl`; `runs/signoff/guard_s0/comparison.json`). An agent that needs a minute per tool call cannot drive hundreds of router moves, so each tool call must carry a whole plan: `apply_plan` executes a list of rip-up and route steps under a checkpoint and returns one result table. Anthropic gives the same advice in general form: a few workflow-level tools that return compact, high-signal results, with limits enforced in the handler ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/writing-tools-for-agents)). The roles that fit a hosted agent are the ones that need a long, self-directed loop or an independent actor. The sign-off agent reproduced your analog judge within 0.0001 dB, and the red-team agent found six confirmed flaws including a real bug, both measured today. Anthropic's finding supports the separation: agents grading their own work skew positive, and a standalone skeptical evaluator is far easier to tune ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)).

**The client must own budgets and state.** Google documents `max_total_tokens` as a best-effort limit per interaction that excludes cached tokens ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/antigravity-agent.md.txt)). Every tool round trip is a new interaction, so a 20-turn run gets 20 budgets: the red-team run's per-turn totals sum to 5.58 million tokens against a 350,000 setting (`runs/redteam/rt1/interactions.jsonl`). Keep a running total on the client and stop answering tool calls when it is spent. Each run forks its own environment, and nothing in the documentation covers two interactions sharing one, so give each agent its own and keep the scoreboard on the laptop ([Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/custom-agents.md)). Use streaming: every failure reported on Google's forum involved background polling or cancel, and both local runs streamed without incident ([forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/managed-antigravity-agent-setup-is-excessively-complex-auth-inconsistencies-stuck-interactions-unusable-cancellation-and-unclear-billing/185863)).

**Develop on one set and report on another.** The one detailed public account of a hardware design swarm, Architect Labs' Frostbite processor, held 15 of 25 workloads out of the search. Its tuned set scored 6.02 times the baseline and the held-out set 3.83 times, and its agents' first planning models were 40% to 130% off ([Architect Labs](https://architectlabs.com/blog/frostbite)). Those numbers are self-reported and unverified. The discipline costs you nothing: 14 hard development boards are prepared, so all prompt changes happen there and the 10 test boards run once.

## What the record says does not work

The measured failures of multi-agent systems are mostly design failures. In 1,642 annotated traces, system-design and specification problems account for 44.2% of failures, misalignment between agents for 32.3% and verification for 23.5%. The commonest single modes are **step repetition (15.7%)** and not knowing when to stop (12.4%); adding one verification step against the task objective raised task success by 15.6% ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). That is the reason for de-duplicating plans against the history and for a hard stop on every job.

| Pattern | What happened | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Equal peers sharing a locked file | 20 agents delivered the throughput of 2 or 3 | [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/scaling-agents) |
| One agent holding many roles | It slept, did the work itself, and declared success early | [Cursor](https://cursor.com/blog/self-driving-codebases) |
| Many agents on one indivisible task | 16 agents hit the same bug and overwrote each other until the task was split | [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler) |
| More agents under a fixed budget | Reasoning per agent becomes too thin beyond 3 or 4; returns turn negative once a single agent already succeeds about 45% of the time | [Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.08296v2) |
| A model emitting coordinates or single steps | 0.19 against 0.65 on the easy tier; 0 of 10 on the hard tier at 676.6 calls per board | [PCBWorld](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05915); [PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2) |
| Self-grading | Evaluators found real issues and then approved the work anyway | [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps) |
| Background mode on the Gemini agent | 404 on GET after a successful create; runs stuck in progress; cancel failing | [forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/antigravity-preview-09-2026-background-interaction-returns-404-immediately-after-successful-creation/184054) |
| Scheduled re-plans on the strong model | Never landed under API concurrency | Measured today |

Four further things are not worth building today. Debate or voting over repair plans has only negative evidence, on question-answering tasks and read from a search summary, and it adds nothing when the scorer answers exactly ([Zhang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08788)). A Managed Agent per worker multiplies a one-minute round trip for a job that is a single decision. Angle repair as a core stage is a mistake: the local scorer counts track angles in a separate column that does not gate clean pass, and each re-route risks a real error (PCBWorld `eval/metrics.py`, lines 435 to 465). Acceptance by a vision model is the fourth: the one modality ablation found, in a chip-placement system, shows image and text inputs within 0.8% of each other, and PCBRouteAgent has no image ablation ([VeoPlace](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28733)).

Gaming of the evaluator is common enough to design against. One 2026 benchmark reports reward hacking in 57.1% of runs across seven frontier agents ([BAITBENCH](https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-emnlp.2477.pdf), search summary only). Your defences are already structural, and the pitch should name them: agents never see the human-routed board, no agent writes the scorer or the scoreboard, and the submitted board is re-scored in a clean process. One loose end remains. The `score` tool returns a `_keys` list of scorer field names; strip it before any hosted agent sees it, and restrict `open`, `save` and `score` to paths the handler chooses.

## Disclosures, conflicts and thin evidence

Say these things on the results slide. The method is a hybrid: Freerouting routes and Gemini repairs. The swarm submits one board per instance chosen by the official scorer from up to 16 counted attempts, while PCBRouteAgent submits one board after up to 12 replans; give attempts, wall-clock time and tokens per board beside its published 516 seconds and $0.39 per hard board on Haiku ([PCBRouteAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.08175v2)). With 10 boards, **one board is 10 points**: 4 of 10 is a tie, 5 of 10 is one board ahead on one run, and neither is a statistical result. The published hard-tier rows are the PCBRouteAgent authors' own local runs, and the human reference itself fails one board. Quote your own Freerouting number, not the published 0.300, as the baseline. Report the control with equal attempts in the adjacent row. Name which parts are Managed Agents and which are plain model calls. State that prompts were tuned on 14 development boards and the test boards ran once.

Do not claim that images help unless an image-off arm on the development boards shows it; say that the image is the planner's input. Do not claim Decisions API use unless the call is wired in today. Do not claim the scheduled re-plans, the audit of the PCB glue, or the PCB sign-off unless each one actually ran; the analog sign-off and red-team runs are real and can be shown as such.

The sources disagree in four places. Cursor's own posts conflict on whether a dedicated merge agent helps (a bottleneck in January, the fix in July) and on whether to keep a judge; neither matters here because attempts never merge. Anthropic ran 16 agents on claim files without an orchestrator, while Cursor's lock file failed at 20; the difference is scale and mechanical enforcement. Anthropic's 90.2% gain from a lead with subagents and Kim et al.'s average loss of 3.5% are both correct, and task shape reconciles them. The fourth is a tension inside this plan: planner quality is the hard-tier lever, which argues for Carbon everywhere, while Carbon's latency and timeouts argue for Hydrogen. The plan resolves it by letting Hydrogen set the pace and treating Carbon as a bounded bonus, and today's logs will show whether Carbon's plans win more often.

Evidence is thin or absent on the points that matter most. No measurement exists of model-driven repair of Freerouting output on the hard tier, so the expected gain is unknown and zero is a possible outcome. No source documents how many hosted Gemini agents can run at once; one [forum report](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/question-about-free-tier-model-availability-for-gemini-4-argon-and-carbon-in-the-antigravity-managed-agent-api/199769) of 429 errors on the free tier this month was seen only in a search summary, so start at two and watch. None of the PCB Managed Agent patterns has been run; the build times in the schedule are judgment. Whether an image returned from a client tool reaches the hosted agent is untested. No published measurement supports a separate triage model inside a swarm, so the Decisions screen is justified by the judging criteria and its low cost, not by evidence. Whether Freerouting's violations on these boards are real collisions or conversion artefacts is unknown, and the first classification of your own ten boards will answer it. Several figures above were extracted by a summarizing fetch tool, so re-check any number before it goes on a slide.

## Conclusion

The research and your own measurements converge on one division of labour. Width belongs to cheap, independent attempts with an exact judge. Depth belongs to one strong actor per hard case. Trust belongs to an actor that did not do the work. Gemini's platform enforces part of this by accident: with no subagent delegation and a one-minute tool round trip, a hosted agent can only be a specialist or an auditor, and the local process has to be the manager. That constraint makes the Managed Agents story cleaner, because each agent has one job with a measured outcome.

The asset that outlasts today is the pairing of a tool that can remove accepted copper with a held-out set of boards. Every published agent on this benchmark either cannot delete routes or will not revisit them, and the hard tier's failures are a handful of trapped connections per board. If two boards flip beyond an equal-attempt control, that is a first Gemini result on a tier where the best published system passes four. If none flip, the two-row table with the control is still an honest finding about where rip-up choice alone runs out, and the next step is clear: give the planner the blocker lists and legal via sites that PCBRouteAgent computes and this tool does not yet.
